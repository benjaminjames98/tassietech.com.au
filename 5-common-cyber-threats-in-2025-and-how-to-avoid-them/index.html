Common Cyber Threats to Watch and How to Avoid Them

The most common cyber threats are still best reduced by practical controls: MFA, password management, patching, backups, segmentation, and reporting.

Cybersecurity 25 Feb 2025 4 min read

Cyber threats change every year, but the most damaging attacks still tend to exploit familiar weaknesses: rushed decisions, weak accounts, unpatched systems, exposed remote access, and unclear recovery plans. The tools used by attackers become more sophisticated, especially with AI-assisted phishing and faster automation, but the defensive work remains surprisingly practical.

The aim is not to chase every headline. A small business gets better results by understanding the most likely threats and building controls that reduce several risks at once. The ACSC scam guidance and CISA's information on malware, phishing, and ransomware are useful references because they focus on patterns that affect ordinary people and organisations.

1. AI-assisted phishing

Phishing no longer needs obvious spelling mistakes or clumsy formatting. Attackers can use AI to create cleaner messages, imitate writing styles, and tailor scams from public information. A fake invoice, parcel notice, Microsoft sign-in alert, or supplier email may look professional enough to pass a quick glance.

The defence is process. Payments should require verification through a trusted channel. MFA prompts should never be approved out of habit. Staff should know how to report suspicious messages without feeling foolish. Good email filtering helps, but people still need permission to slow down.

2. Account takeover

When attackers take over email or cloud accounts, they gain access to conversations, documents, password resets, and trusted relationships. Microsoft 365, Dropbox, WordPress, remote support tools, and banking portals are all high-value targets because they can lead to more systems.

Use MFA, avoid password reuse, and store credentials in 1Password or another reputable password manager. Review sign-in logs, forwarding rules, shared inboxes, and administrator roles. A compromised account should be treated as a wider investigation, not just a password reset.

3. Ransomware and extortion

Ransomware is no longer only about encrypted files. Many incidents include data theft and pressure to pay. Attackers may target backups, remote access, administrator accounts, and file shares before the business realises anything is wrong.

Prepare with protected backups, restricted admin rights, network segmentation, patched systems, and tested restore procedures. A Synology NAS can support local backup and snapshots, while Microsoft 365 and Dropbox require their own recovery planning. The goal is to reduce both the chance of infection and the pressure if one occurs.

4. Weak remote access

Remote access is essential for many teams, but poorly configured access is a common pathway into business systems. Exposed remote desktop, shared support logins, old VPN accounts, and tools without MFA can all create unnecessary risk.

Use named accounts, MFA, least privilege, logging, and regular reviews. Splashtop can be a strong support tool when it is configured deliberately. It should not become a permanent open door that nobody checks.

5. Website and plugin compromise

Small-business websites are often built and then ignored. WordPress core, themes, plugins, admin accounts, hosting access, and backups all need maintenance. A compromised website can redirect visitors, send spam, host malicious files, or expose form submissions.

Keep the site patched, remove unused plugins, use MFA where available, store admin credentials properly, and make sure hosting responsibilities are clear. If the site runs on managed infrastructure such as Synergy Web Servers, that still needs a documented maintenance owner and tested backup process.

What ties the controls together

The same controls reduce several threats at once: MFA, password management, patching, backups, network segmentation, staff reporting, and clean administration. Security becomes more manageable when those controls are part of normal operations rather than emergency projects.
