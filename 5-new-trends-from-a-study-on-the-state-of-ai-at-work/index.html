New Technology 20 Dec 2024 4 min read

The most interesting AI trend at work is not that more tools exist. It is that AI is moving from occasional experimentation into ordinary workflows: meeting notes, email drafting, search, spreadsheet analysis, customer responses, and internal knowledge management. That shift creates a new kind of management problem. Leaders are no longer deciding whether AI exists in the workplace. They are deciding whether it is governed or informal.

The Stanford AI Index tracks how quickly AI capability and adoption are changing, while the NIST AI Risk Management Framework gives organisations a way to think about trust, privacy, security, and accountability. Taken together, they point to a practical conclusion: AI adoption should be deliberate, not accidental.

Trend 1: Staff are already experimenting

Many staff try AI before the business has a policy. They may use it to draft emails, summarise documents, write formulas, generate social posts, or troubleshoot software. That experimentation is understandable, but unmanaged use can expose customer details, internal documents, or confidential strategy to tools the business has not reviewed.

The response should not be panic or a blanket ban. Give people approved tools, clear examples of acceptable use, and a simple path to request new tools. If Microsoft 365 is already the operating environment, review whether AI features inside that ecosystem provide better governance than a patchwork of separate subscriptions.

Trend 2: Data quality matters more than prompt skill

AI outputs depend heavily on the data available to the tool. If SharePoint is disorganised, permissions are too broad, and documents are outdated, AI can produce faster confusion. A beautifully written answer based on stale policy is still wrong.

Before rolling out AI search or summarisation, clean up document ownership, external sharing, retention, and naming. AI governance often starts with information management, not model selection.

Trend 3: Meeting records are becoming business records

AI note-taking makes meetings easier to search and summarise, but it also creates transcripts, summaries, action items, and sometimes recordings. Those artefacts may include sensitive HR, finance, health, or client information. The business needs rules for when AI notes are allowed, where they are stored, who can access them, and when they should be deleted.

Trend 4: AI is reshaping entry-level work

Tasks that once helped junior staff learn, such as drafting, summarising, research, and formatting, can now be accelerated by AI. That is useful, but it changes training. People still need to learn how to check sources, recognise weak reasoning, ask better questions, and understand the customer context.

The mature workflow is human plus AI, not AI instead of judgement. Staff should be encouraged to show what they checked, not just what the tool produced.

Trend 5: Trust is becoming a competitive advantage

Clients and staff will increasingly ask how AI is used. Does the business paste client information into public tools? Are outputs reviewed? Are meeting recordings disclosed? Are passwords, API keys, and automation credentials stored safely? Can AI access documents it should not?

Good answers build confidence. Keep credentials in 1Password, control Microsoft 365 permissions, review SharePoint libraries, document approved tools, and make AI use transparent when it affects clients. AI can improve productivity, but trust is what makes that productivity sustainable.
