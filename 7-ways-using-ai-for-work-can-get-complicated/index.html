AI at Work: 7 Risks Small Teams Should Plan For

AI at work is useful when it is governed: approved tools, clear data rules, permission reviews, and human checking matter more than hype.

AI tools can be genuinely useful at work. They can summarise long documents, turn rough notes into drafts, compare ideas, generate formulas, and help people move faster through routine tasks. The complication is that work data is rarely neutral. It may include customer details, staff information, financial records, passwords, strategy, confidential emails, or documents that only certain people should see.

That is why AI adoption should be treated as an operating decision, not just a software trend. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework encourages organisations to think about validity, privacy, security, transparency, and accountability before relying on AI outputs. For a small business, that does not mean building a bureaucracy. It means deciding which tools are approved, what data can be entered, who checks the output, and how mistakes are handled.

1. The tool may not protect data the way staff assume

People often paste information into an AI tool because it feels like a search box or writing assistant. In a business context, that habit needs boundaries. Customer names, contracts, health information, financial documents, credentials, and internal strategy should not be entered into consumer tools unless the business has reviewed the terms and data handling.

When AI is used inside the Microsoft ecosystem, it is worth understanding the difference between consumer AI and business tools. Microsoft explains its approach to enterprise data protection for Microsoft 365 Copilot, including how organisational data is handled in that environment. That kind of documentation is exactly what a business should look for before approving any AI tool.

2. AI can make incorrect work look polished

AI-generated text often sounds confident even when it is wrong, incomplete, or missing context. That is dangerous in client advice, policy writing, financial analysis, legal material, technical documentation, and anything that affects safety or compliance. A tidy paragraph is not the same as a verified answer.

The practical rule is simple: use AI to accelerate drafting and thinking, not to remove human responsibility. The person sending the email, publishing the page, or making the decision still owns the accuracy.

3. Permissions can become invisible

AI search and summarisation can surface information from places people forgot existed. In Microsoft 365, this makes SharePoint and OneDrive permissions more important, not less. If a site has years of inherited access, old external links, or broad sharing groups, AI can make those permission mistakes more visible.

Before rolling out AI across a team, review document libraries, sensitive folders, and external sharing. A well-structured SharePoint environment gives AI better guardrails and gives staff more confidence that search results reflect what they are actually allowed to see.

4. Shadow AI creates scattered risk

If the business does not provide guidance, staff will choose their own tools. One person may use a browser extension, another may use a free chatbot, another may upload documents to a design assistant, and another may connect an AI note-taker to meetings. Each tool may have different privacy terms, retention settings, and admin visibility.

A lightweight AI policy helps. It should list approved tools, banned data types, review expectations, and the contact person for new requests. Store any AI service passwords or recovery details in 1Password rather than personal notes or shared spreadsheets.

5. Meeting tools can capture more than expected

AI meeting summaries are useful, but they can also record sensitive conversations by default. Before enabling them, decide whether participants need notice, where transcripts are stored, who can access them, and how long they are retained. This matters for HR, legal, medical, financial, and client discussions.

6. Outputs can reflect bias or missing context

AI tools learn from data and patterns, not from your organisation's judgement. They may suggest generic advice that does not suit Tasmania, your industry, your customers, or your service model. Treat AI output as a draft that needs local context, not as an authority.

7. The real gain comes from workflow design

The best AI use is usually modest and repeatable: summarising a long vendor document, drafting a first-pass FAQ, cleaning up meeting notes, building a spreadsheet formula, or creating a starting point for a policy. These tasks save time without handing over final judgement.

AI becomes complicated when it is adopted tool by tool without rules. It becomes useful when it is placed inside existing controls: Microsoft 365 permissions, SharePoint structure, 1Password credential management, backup practices, and a clear review process. That balance lets a team use AI without pretending it is risk-free.
