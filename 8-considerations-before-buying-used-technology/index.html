Buying Used Technology: 8 Checks Before You Spend

Used technology can be good value, but only when support life, reset status, security, battery health, and management fit the job.

Used technology can be a smart purchase, especially when the budget is tight or the device only needs to handle a clear job. A refurbished laptop for reception, a second-hand iPad for bookings, or a pre-owned desktop for light office work can be perfectly sensible. The problem is that used devices are rarely just cheaper versions of new devices. They come with unknown history, shorter support windows, worn components, and a higher chance that someone else's settings or accounts are still attached.

A good second-hand purchase is therefore less about luck and more about due diligence. Before money changes hands, treat the device like a small business asset: check whether it can still be updated, whether it can be reset cleanly, whether the battery and storage are healthy, and whether it will fit into the way you actually work. The ACSC small business cyber security guide puts updates, backups, and MFA among the basics for reducing common cyber risk. A used device that cannot support those basics is not a bargain.

Support life matters more than age

The first question is not simply, "How old is it?" The better question is, "How long will it keep receiving security updates?" A Windows PC might look modern but fail to meet Windows 11 requirements. A phone might still work but no longer receive operating system updates. A router or NAS might be fast enough but abandoned by the vendor. Once updates stop, the risk slowly rises even if the device still turns on every morning.

For business use, that support window should line up with your expected lifespan. If you need the device for three years, do not buy hardware that is already near the end of support unless the role is low risk and isolated. For devices that will access Microsoft 365, SharePoint, Dropbox, remote desktop, accounting tools, or customer files, modern patching and management support are not optional details.

Account locks and licensing can ruin the deal

A used device should be able to be fully reset before you take ownership. Check for Apple Activation Lock, Microsoft account lock-in, mobile device management profiles, unknown administrator passwords, missing Windows activation, and software that is installed but not licensed to the new owner. Apple's own guidance on Activation Lock is a useful reminder that a device can be physically in your hands but still tied to someone else's account.

Ask the seller to show the reset process, not just the desktop screen. If the device is for a business, start from a fresh build and enrol it properly into your normal setup: Microsoft 365, endpoint protection, 1Password, backup policies, and remote support tools such as Splashtop where appropriate. Do not inherit a previous owner's browser profile, saved passwords, VPN clients, or half-removed business software.

Condition is more than scratches

Physical wear is easy to see; component wear is not. On a laptop, check battery cycle count, keyboard, trackpad, webcam, microphone, speakers, hinges, USB-C ports, charger condition, display brightness, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and whether the fan is unusually loud. On a desktop, check storage health, dust, ports, noise, and whether the power supply is appropriate. On a NAS, check drive age and SMART status rather than assuming all disks are equally healthy.

A cheap device can quickly become expensive if it needs a battery, SSD, charger, dock, RAM upgrade, or screen repair. Compare the real total against a new entry-level device with warranty. A refurbished business-class machine can be excellent value, but only when the remaining life and repair cost make sense.

Where used technology works well

Used hardware is usually safest when the job is narrow and the data risk is low. A spare monitor, keyboard, dock, test machine, lab device, or community-use laptop can be a good candidate. A second-hand device that will store sensitive client information, run payroll, administer WordPress, manage UniFi networking, or hold the only copy of important files needs a much higher standard.

If the device will become part of a managed workplace, make sure it can be supported cleanly. That means current operating system support, encryption, MFA-capable accounts, a backup path for local files, and clear ownership in your asset list. Saving a few hundred dollars is not worth creating an unmanaged exception that the team forgets about until it breaks.

A simple buying rule

Buy used technology when it is supportable, resettable, repairable, and right-sized for the task. Walk away when the seller cannot prove ownership, the device cannot be updated, the battery or storage is near failure, or the price only looks good because hidden costs have been ignored. The best second-hand device is not the cheapest one. It is the one you can trust enough to put into service without making your environment harder to maintain.
