Cyber Hygiene Checklist for Safer Everyday Technology

Good cyber hygiene is a repeatable routine: protect identities, patch quiet systems, clean permissions, test backups, segment networks, and normalise reporting.

Cybersecurity 20 Dec 2024 4 min read

Cyber hygiene is the security equivalent of routine maintenance. It is not a single product, a once-a-year audit, or a poster in the staff room. It is the set of habits that keeps accounts, devices, files, networks, websites, and backups from drifting into avoidable risk. The work is rarely glamorous, but it is what prevents small mistakes from becoming expensive incidents.

For small businesses, the most useful cyber hygiene program is practical and repeatable. The ACSC small business cyber security guide and the Australian Government's Essential Eight both point toward the same foundations: patch systems, restrict access, protect accounts, back up data, and prepare for recovery.

Make identity the first control

Email and cloud accounts are often the front door to everything else. If an attacker controls a mailbox, they can reset passwords, read invoices, impersonate staff, and search for sensitive documents. That is why MFA should be enabled for Microsoft 365, email, banking, password managers, WordPress, Dropbox, remote support, NAS administration, and network equipment.

Use 1Password or another reputable password manager so every account can have a unique password. For teams, shared vaults should be structured by role. Not everyone needs access to every admin credential, and former staff should not remain in shared vaults after they leave.

Patch the systems people forget

Most people remember laptop updates eventually. The weaker spots are often browsers, phone apps, WordPress plugins, NAS firmware, routers, UniFi controllers, printer firmware, remote access tools, and old software that only one person uses. Build a monthly patch review that includes the quiet systems, not just the obvious ones.

If a device or application can no longer receive updates, decide whether it should be replaced, isolated, or retired. Unsupported systems may still function, but they become harder to defend over time.

Keep file access tidy

Permissions drift. SharePoint libraries that began with three users become available to a whole department. Dropbox folders stay shared with old contractors. NAS shares keep groups that no longer exist. Website administrators remain active long after a project ends.

Schedule access reviews for Microsoft 365, SharePoint, OneDrive, Dropbox, WordPress, Synergy Web Servers, Synology NAS devices, Splashtop, and UniFi administration. Remove old users, reduce admin rights, and use named accounts wherever possible. Least privilege is not about mistrust; it is about limiting damage when something goes wrong.

Backups need evidence

A backup that has never been restored is not proven. Review backups for files, Microsoft 365 data, SharePoint libraries, WordPress sites, databases, NAS folders, and key application exports. Make sure at least one copy is protected from ransomware or accidental deletion. A Synology NAS can be a useful local backup target, but it should be patched, monitored, and paired with offsite protection.

Test recovery on a schedule. Restore a file, a folder, a mailbox item, and a website backup. Write down who can do it and how long it takes. That evidence is what makes recovery planning real.

Separate networks by trust

Guest Wi-Fi, smart devices, staff devices, servers, and backups do not all need the same level of access. Network segmentation limits the effect of a compromised device. UniFi networking hardware can make segmentation manageable through guest networks, VLANs, firewall rules, and device visibility.

This is especially useful where IoT devices, cameras, TVs, and printers share space with business laptops. Convenience should not mean every device can reach everything.

Make reporting normal

People will click suspicious links, lose devices, approve strange prompts, and share files too broadly. The quality of the response matters. Staff should know who to contact and should not feel punished for reporting quickly. A fast report can prevent a small incident from spreading.

Cyber hygiene works because it is routine. Review accounts, patch systems, check backups, clean permissions, and keep the network segmented. Do it repeatedly and calmly, and the business becomes harder to disrupt.
