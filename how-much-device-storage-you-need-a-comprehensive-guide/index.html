How Much Storage Do You Need on a Laptop, Phone, or Work PC?

The right storage size depends on offline work, shared cloud storage, local files, and backup strategy rather than raw capacity alone.

Storage decisions used to be simple: buy the biggest drive you could afford. That is no longer the best rule. Many files now live in cloud services, photos and videos are larger than ever, and business data may be spread across laptops, Microsoft 365, SharePoint, OneDrive, Dropbox, a Synology NAS, and website hosting. The right amount of storage depends less on the device itself and more on the way your data moves.

A good storage plan answers three questions. What needs to be available on the device when you are offline? What should live in shared cloud storage so the team can collaborate? What needs a proper backup rather than simple sync? Microsoft explains that SharePoint and OneDrive include strong cloud protections, but that does not remove the need for sensible permissions, retention, versioning, and backup planning on the customer side.

Personal devices: leave room for the future

For a basic phone or tablet used mostly for browsing, messaging, and a modest photo library, 128 GB can be comfortable. If you shoot lots of video, download media for travel, play large games, or keep work files offline, 256 GB or more is safer. Storage pressure often appears slowly: the device works well for a year, then updates, photos, cache files, and app data start competing for space.

On laptops, 256 GB is now the practical minimum for light use, but 512 GB is a better baseline for most people. Anyone handling photo libraries, design files, virtual machines, CAD, video, or large offline folders should think in terabytes. Running a laptop permanently near full capacity can slow updates, break sync, and make recovery harder.

Business devices: separate working space from shared storage

A work laptop should have enough local storage for the operating system, applications, cached cloud files, current projects, and recovery headroom. It should not become the only place important business files exist. Shared documents belong in managed locations such as SharePoint, OneDrive, or Dropbox where permissions, version history, and collaboration are easier to control.

That distinction matters. If every staff member keeps their own copies of key documents on local drives, the business ends up with duplicates, stale versions, and unclear ownership. If everything is synced without rules, a mistake or ransomware event can spread quickly. Good storage design is about access and recovery, not just capacity.

When a NAS makes sense

A Synology NAS can be useful when a site works with large local files, needs fast local access, runs local backups, or wants a controlled place for shared storage. It can be a strong part of a backup and file strategy, especially for offices with design files, media libraries, scans, archives, or datasets that are awkward to keep entirely in the cloud.

A NAS is not a magic backup box. It needs healthy disks, monitoring, updates, permissions, snapshots where appropriate, and an offsite copy. Synology's own data backup guidance is useful because it frames NAS backup as part of a broader strategy rather than a single destination.

Cloud sync is not the same as backup

Cloud storage is excellent for access and collaboration. Backup is about recovery after deletion, corruption, ransomware, account compromise, or a bad change. Those are related but different goals. The ACSC small business guide includes backups as one of the core controls because recovery depends on having a copy that is protected from the incident that damaged the original.

For Microsoft 365, SharePoint, Dropbox, WordPress, and hosted systems, confirm what is retained, how long deleted items can be recovered, and what happens if an account is compromised. Do not assume that sync history is enough for business continuity.

A practical storage rule

Choose device storage for performance and day-to-day working space. Choose shared storage for collaboration and access control. Choose backup storage for recovery. Those three needs may overlap, but they should not be confused. A well-sized laptop, a tidy SharePoint structure, a properly configured Dropbox workspace, and a monitored Synology NAS can all be part of the same plan when each has a clear role.

If you are unsure, start by listing the files that would stop work if they disappeared tomorrow. That list will tell you more about your storage needs than the advertised size of any device.
