Ransomware Response: How to Limit the Damage

Ransomware resilience depends on limiting entry, restricting spread, protecting backups, and practising the first-hour response before an incident.

Ransomware preparation is sometimes described as prevention, but prevention is only half the story. The better question is: if ransomware reached one device today, how far could it spread, what data could it reach, and how quickly could the business recover? That shift turns ransomware from a vague fear into an operational planning exercise.

The CISA ransomware guide and the ACSC's guidance on recovering from ransomware both point to the same reality: recovery depends on preparation done before the incident. Clean backups, restricted access, patching, logging, and a clear response process make the difference between disruption and disaster.

Limit the first foothold

Many ransomware incidents begin with a stolen password, phishing email, exposed remote access service, malicious attachment, or unpatched system. MFA on email, Microsoft 365, remote desktop, VPN, WordPress, and admin portals is one of the simplest ways to make stolen passwords less useful. A password manager such as 1Password also reduces reuse, which limits the damage from one compromised account.

Patch management matters here as well. Windows, macOS, browsers, Microsoft 365 apps, remote support tools, NAS firmware, UniFi controllers, WordPress plugins, and hosting panels should all sit inside a maintenance rhythm. Attackers do not need every system to be vulnerable. They only need one good entry point.

Stop one device becoming the whole network

Flat networks make ransomware more dangerous because every device can see too much. Segmenting guest Wi-Fi, IoT devices, servers, NAS storage, and staff devices limits movement. The goal is not to create complexity for its own sake. It is to make sure a compromised laptop cannot automatically reach backups, cameras, file shares, and admin consoles.

UniFi networking hardware can help small offices apply that separation in a manageable way, especially where guest networks, VLANs, and device visibility are needed. The configuration still matters: firewall rules, admin access, firmware updates, and monitoring need to be maintained.

Protect backups from the same attack

Backups are only useful if ransomware cannot encrypt or delete them at the same time as the originals. That means using separate credentials, offsite or cloud copies, immutable or snapshot-based protection where appropriate, and regular restore testing. A Synology NAS can play a strong role with snapshots and local backup speed, but it should not be the only copy and it should not use the same everyday admin credentials as staff workstations.

Microsoft 365, SharePoint, OneDrive, and Dropbox can provide version history and retention features, but those are not a complete ransomware recovery plan by themselves. Know what can be restored, how far back it goes, and who has the authority to restore it.

Write the first-hour response before you need it

During an incident, people lose time deciding who is in charge. Prepare a short response plan that says who isolates devices, who contacts IT support, who contacts management, who preserves evidence, who talks to insurers or legal advisers, and who communicates with staff. The plan should also say what not to do, such as deleting evidence, rebuilding systems too early, or reconnecting devices before the scope is understood.

For small businesses, this can be a one-page document stored somewhere accessible outside the affected network. Include key phone numbers, cyber insurance details, hosting contacts, Microsoft 365 administrator contacts, and backup recovery steps.

Practise recovery, not just backup

A backup dashboard showing green checks is not the same as proof of recovery. Test restoring a SharePoint library, a file share, a WordPress site, a database, and a full workstation if those systems matter to the business. Time the process and write down the gaps.

Ransomware damage is minimised when the business can contain, understand, restore, and communicate. The controls are not glamorous, but they are practical: MFA, patching, segmentation, protected backups, clean admin access, and rehearsed recovery.
