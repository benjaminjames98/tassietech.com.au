Data Breach Notice? 8 Steps to Protect Yourself

A breach notice needs a calm response: confirm the notice, secure passwords and MFA, check accounts, watch identity risk, and expect follow-on scams.

A data breach notice creates two problems at once. First, some of your information may already be outside your control. Second, scammers may use the notice itself as a reason to contact you with fake refunds, fake support calls, fake identity checks, or urgent password-reset links. The safest response is calm, ordered, and sceptical of anything that tries to rush you.

In Australia, the OAIC's Notifiable Data Breaches information explains how eligible data breaches are handled, while Scamwatch and IDCARE are useful references if the breach increases scam or identity-theft risk. You do not need to solve everything in one minute, but you do need to start with the accounts that could cause the most harm.

1. Confirm the notice without clicking through

Read the breach notice, but avoid using links or phone numbers inside unexpected messages until you have checked them. Go to the organisation's website directly, use an official app, or call a known public number. Scammers often imitate real breach notices because they know people are anxious.

Look for the type of data involved: email address, phone number, password, payment details, identity document, health information, address, date of birth, or account activity. The response depends on what was exposed.

2. Change the affected password and any reused passwords

If the breached service used a password, change it immediately from a clean device. If that password was reused anywhere else, change those accounts too. Reuse is the reason one breach becomes many takeovers.

A password manager such as 1Password makes this easier because it can generate unique passwords and help you find reused ones. Start with email, Microsoft 365, banking, social media, cloud storage, Dropbox, WordPress, and any account that can reset other accounts.

3. Turn on MFA where it matters most

MFA should be enabled on the breached account if available and on important accounts that could be targeted next. Email deserves special priority because password reset links often flow through it. Use an authenticator app, passkey, or security key where possible. SMS is better than no MFA, but stronger methods are preferable for critical accounts.

4. Review account activity and recovery settings

Check recent sign-ins, connected apps, forwarding rules, recovery email addresses, phone numbers, and trusted devices. This is particularly important for Microsoft 365, Gmail, Apple ID, Dropbox, banking, and social accounts. Remove anything unfamiliar and record anything suspicious before deleting it.

If the account is used for work, report the notice internally. Administrators may need to review sign-in logs, SharePoint access, mailbox rules, remote sessions, or device compliance.

5. Watch money and identity channels

If payment details or identity documents were involved, contact your bank or relevant provider for advice. Watch transactions, new account notices, credit applications, SIM-swap warnings, and unexpected identity checks. Keep notes of dates, reference numbers, and screenshots of suspicious activity.

If identity information was exposed, IDCARE can help people work through tailored response steps. The right action may depend on the document type and how much information was included in the breach.

6. Expect follow-on scams

After a breach, scammers may contact you pretending to be the affected organisation, your bank, a government agency, a courier, or technical support. They may know enough real details to sound convincing. Be wary of urgency, secrecy, payment requests, remote-access requests, or requests for verification codes.

Do not install remote access software because a caller tells you to. Do not read out MFA codes. If you think you may have interacted with a scam, use our scam recovery guide and act quickly.

7. Update devices and security tools

Keep your operating system, browser, password manager, email app, and security software current. If you clicked a suspicious link, opened an attachment, or installed anything after receiving a breach-related message, have the device checked before continuing to use it for banking or password changes.

8. Keep monitoring after the first week

Some misuse happens months later, especially when identity details are involved. Keep watching for unusual account activity, reset emails, payment changes, unfamiliar devices, or unexpected calls. Save the breach notice and your response notes in a safe place so you can refer back if something appears later.

A breach notice is not something to ignore, but it is also not a reason to panic. Confirm the notice, secure the accounts, strengthen MFA, watch for financial and identity misuse, and treat follow-on messages with care.
