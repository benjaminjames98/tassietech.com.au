Mobile App Security: 10 Checks Before You Tap

Mobile app security improves when app permissions, passwords, MFA, updates, Wi-Fi habits, and cloud sync are treated as one system.

Cybersecurity 20 Apr 2025 4 min read

Mobile apps are now part of banking, health care, travel, messaging, work, delivery, entertainment, and identity checks. That makes phones convenient, but it also means a compromised phone can expose more than photos and messages. A malicious app, stolen password, weak lock screen, or careless approval prompt can affect email, Microsoft 365, Dropbox, banking, social media, and personal documents at the same time.

The good news is that mobile security is usually improved by a handful of habits repeated consistently. The ACSC small business guide emphasises MFA, software updates, and backups as practical starting points. Those same ideas apply neatly to mobile apps, whether you are protecting a personal phone or a staff device used for work.

1. Install apps from trusted stores

Use the Apple App Store or Google Play unless there is a very specific reason not to. Official stores do not remove all risk, but they reduce the chance of installing a tampered app from an unknown website. Be especially careful with apps promoted through urgent text messages, social media ads, or pop-ups claiming that your device is infected.

2. Read permissions like a risk signal

A weather app should not need your contacts. A calculator should not need microphone access. Some permissions are legitimate, but permissions should match the job the app performs. If an app asks for broad access before it has earned trust, pause and look for another option.

3. Use a password manager, not repeated passwords

Passwords reused across apps are one of the fastest ways for a breach to spread. NIST's password advice encourages longer, stronger passwords and avoiding predictable patterns; its guide on creating good passwords is a useful plain-English reference. A password manager such as 1Password makes unique passwords realistic because you do not need to remember each one.

4. Turn on MFA for important apps

Email, banking, Microsoft 365, password managers, cloud storage, and social media should all use multi-factor authentication where available. MFA is particularly important on mobile because many apps stay signed in for long periods. A stolen password should not be enough to open the door.

5. Keep the phone and apps updated

App updates often fix security problems, not just bugs. Operating system updates do the same. If a phone is too old to receive updates, it should not be trusted with high-risk accounts. For businesses, unsupported phones should be treated like unsupported laptops: remove work access or replace the device.

6. Be careful with public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi is convenient, but it is not a trusted environment. Avoid sensitive tasks on unknown networks unless the app uses strong encryption and you are confident you are on the legitimate service. A better habit is to use mobile data for banking and other sensitive actions when you are away from home or work.

7. Review cloud sync and storage

Many apps automatically save documents, photos, chat exports, or attachments to cloud storage. That can be useful when SharePoint, OneDrive, or Dropbox is part of a managed workflow, but it can also scatter sensitive files into personal accounts. Know where files are going and whether the account is protected with MFA.

8. Remove apps you no longer use

Unused apps still take up space, request updates, hold old data, and sometimes keep account tokens active. Delete apps you no longer need, then close the account if it contains personal information. This is especially important for travel, shopping, finance, and one-off event apps.

9. Use screen lock and device tracking

A strong passcode, biometrics, device encryption, and lost-device tracking are basic protections. They matter because many apps remain signed in. If the phone is lost, you want the ability to lock or erase it quickly before app sessions and saved files are exposed.

10. Slow down when an app creates urgency

Scam messages often push people into installing apps, approving MFA prompts, or entering credentials on fake login pages. The ACSC's scam guidance recommends using trusted contact methods rather than links or numbers supplied in a suspicious message. That habit is simple, but it stops many mobile-led attacks.

Mobile security is not about avoiding apps. It is about choosing them carefully, limiting what they can access, protecting the accounts behind them, and keeping the device healthy enough to trust.
