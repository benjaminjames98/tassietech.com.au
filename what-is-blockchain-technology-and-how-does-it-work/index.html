New Technology 10 Feb 2025 4 min read

Blockchain is one of those technologies that attracts both genuine innovation and heavy exaggeration. At its core, it is a way of maintaining a shared record across multiple participants without relying on a single central database owner. That idea can be useful in narrow situations, but it is not automatically better than a conventional database, contract, audit trail, or payment system.

NIST's Blockchain Technology Overview is a useful neutral reference because it explains the components without treating blockchain as magic. For most small businesses, the practical question is not \"Should we use blockchain?\" It is \"Does this problem genuinely need a shared, tamper-resistant ledger with multiple parties who do not fully trust one another?\"

How a blockchain works in plain language

A blockchain groups transactions or records into blocks. Each block contains data, a timestamp, and a cryptographic reference to the block before it. That chaining makes tampering easier to detect because changing old data would also change the references that follow it. In public blockchains, many participants hold copies of the ledger and use consensus rules to agree on what is valid.

This design can provide transparency and integrity, but it comes with trade-offs. Public blockchains can be slower, more expensive, and less private than conventional systems. Private or permissioned blockchains reduce some of those issues but also reintroduce questions about who controls access and governance.

Where it can make sense

Blockchain can be relevant where multiple parties need a shared record and no single party is fully trusted to maintain it alone. Examples often discussed include supply-chain records, digital assets, provenance tracking, decentralised identity, and some settlement systems. Even then, the technology is only one part of the solution. Data quality, legal agreements, privacy, and integration usually matter more than the ledger itself.

A blockchain cannot verify that a physical product is genuine unless the real-world input process is trustworthy. It cannot make a poor business process fair. It cannot remove the need for security controls around wallets, keys, applications, and people.

Where it usually does not help

Most small-business problems do not need blockchain. File sharing, customer records, booking systems, internal approvals, backups, websites, password management, and invoices are usually better served by mature platforms such as Microsoft 365, SharePoint, Dropbox, WordPress, accounting software, and properly managed hosting. These tools are easier to support, easier to recover, and easier for staff to use.

If a vendor proposes blockchain for an ordinary workflow, ask what problem it solves that a normal database, audit log, digital signature, or permissions model cannot solve. If the answer is vague, the technology may be adding complexity rather than value.

The security issue is often key management

Blockchain systems depend heavily on cryptographic keys. If a private key is lost, access may be permanently lost. If it is stolen, assets or permissions may be abused. That makes password managers, MFA, hardware security, backup processes, and role separation important. The risk is less about the abstract blockchain and more about the way people and applications handle access.

Businesses experimenting with blockchain should document who owns keys, how recovery works, what happens when staff leave, and how transactions are approved. Credentials should not live in personal notes or unmanaged browser profiles.

A sensible business view

Blockchain is a specialised tool. It can be useful when the trust model genuinely calls for it, but it should not distract from higher-value foundations: secure Microsoft 365 accounts, tidy SharePoint permissions, 1Password-managed credentials, maintained WordPress sites, reliable backups, and well-configured network equipment.

For most organisations, the right move is to understand blockchain well enough to assess claims, avoid hype-led purchasing, and recognise the few cases where the technology actually fits.
