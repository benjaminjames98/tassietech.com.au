Threat Exposure Management: Finding IT Gaps Before Attackers Do

Threat exposure management gives businesses a ranked view of risky accounts, assets, public systems, permissions, backups, and network paths.

Threat exposure management is a structured way to ask, "What could an attacker reach from here?" It goes beyond a once-a-year vulnerability scan and looks across assets, accounts, permissions, public services, cloud settings, backups, websites, and network paths. The output should not be a long report that sits unread. It should be a ranked list of exposures the business can actually reduce.

The idea aligns closely with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, which begins with identifying what exists and then protecting, detecting, responding, and recovering. Threat exposure management adds an attacker's view: which gaps are easiest to find, easiest to exploit, and most likely to matter?

Exposure starts with assets

Every review should begin with the systems the business relies on: Microsoft 365, SharePoint, OneDrive, Dropbox, WordPress, Synergy Web Servers, Synology NAS devices, UniFi network equipment, Splashtop, laptops, mobile devices, domain names, DNS, SSL certificates, and vendor portals. Unknown assets are dangerous because nobody is patching, backing up, or monitoring them.

This inventory can be simple at first. The point is to know what exists, who owns it, and how important it is. Over time, the inventory becomes a map for risk decisions.

Permissions are exposure

Attackers often do not need a technical exploit if they can sign in with a valid account. Review old users, guest accounts, shared mailboxes, administrator roles, SharePoint permissions, WordPress admins, NAS shares, and password vault access. Remove what is no longer needed and reduce admin rights wherever possible.

1Password can help by making shared credentials visible and reviewable, but it should sit alongside identity controls rather than replace them. The aim is to know who can access what and why.

Public-facing systems deserve priority

Anything reachable from the internet deserves special attention: websites, remote access portals, VPNs, cloud dashboards, hosting panels, and exposed services. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is a useful reminder that attackers move quickly when widely used products have known flaws.

Patch public-facing systems promptly, remove unused services, enforce MFA, and avoid exposing management interfaces unless there is a clear need. WordPress plugins, hosting panels, routers, firewalls, and NAS devices should not be left to drift.

Backups and recovery are part of exposure

A business with untested backups is more exposed to ransomware, deletion, and mistakes. Check whether Microsoft 365 data, SharePoint libraries, Dropbox folders, WordPress sites, NAS data, and key application exports can be restored. Confirm who has access to backups and whether backup deletion is protected.

Recovery exposure is often invisible until an incident. Threat exposure management brings it into the same conversation as prevention.

Turn the review into action

The final step is prioritisation. A missing patch on an exposed service matters more than a low-risk setting on an isolated test device. A former staff admin account matters more than a cosmetic warning. A failed backup for critical data matters more than a dashboard score.

A useful TEM review ends with owners, due dates, and practical fixes. It should make the business easier to defend, not just more aware of problems.
